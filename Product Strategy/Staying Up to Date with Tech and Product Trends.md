# Staying Up to Date with Tech & Product Trends

> Strategy and product sense questions test whether you've been paying attention to the world your interviewer operates in. The pace of change right now means a few months of inattention show up immediately in your answers. Interviewers want to hire PMs who keep their products ahead of the market. Your answers tell them whether you'd be that person.

---

## Why This Matters Now

Competitive moats that used to take years to build now collapse in months. Interviewers at Frontier AI Labs, FAANG, and unicorn startups are specifically evaluating whether you track the market — because PMs who don't get surprised by the competition on the job.

Beyond awareness, they want to know you **engage with AI products firsthand**, not just follow the news about them. The candidates who stand out in strategy and product sense interviews are not the ones who studied harder the week before. They are the ones who regularly track the market.

> **Watch out:** Staying up to date may show up early. At Google, an L5 PM candidate reported that a competitive strategy question surfaced as early as the hiring manager call.

## The Three Layers

There are three layers to staying current, and they solve three different problems:

| Layer | Name | What It Solves | What It Produces |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Passive intake | Awareness | You know what happened |
| **2** | Active synthesis | Depth | You know what it means |
| **3** | Hands-on fluency | Credibility | You have real opinions |

Interviewers can tell which layer someone is operating from within the first few minutes of a strategy question.

---

## Layer 1 — Passive Intake

*Information that keeps you current without demanding attention. Set it up once and it runs.*

### Social feeds

Search *"AI trends"* and *"tech news"* on LinkedIn, X, and any other platforms you use. Follow 5–10 accounts that post about tech strategy and product. Engage with what resonates. The algorithm updates itself in about two weeks — from then on, ambient exposure is built in without extra time.

### Build your own daily briefing (the most valuable step here)

**Step 1: Open Claude and create a new Project.**
Name it something like *"Interview Prep: Trends."*

In the project instructions, add context about your situation so every response is calibrated to you:

> *"I am preparing for a PM interview at [Company A, Company B, Company C]. My interview is on approximately [date]. When giving me briefings or analysis, always prioritize news and moves that are most relevant to these companies, their competitors, and the AI and product trends they care about most."*

**Step 2: Create a daily scheduled task that delivers a briefing to your email each morning.**

Use this prompt:

```
Search for and deliver my daily interview prep briefing.

Section 1: [Companies I'm interviewing at]
Any news from the last 24 hours: product launches, executive changes, earnings, 
partnerships, regulatory actions, or anything a PM candidate should know before 
walking in.

Section 2: FAANG and major AI company moves
What did major tech companies do strategically in the last 24 hours? Prioritize 
product launches, acquisitions, partnerships, and competitive moves. For each item: 
one sentence on what happened, one sentence on why it matters.

Section 3: Policy and legal
What happened in AI regulation, legal cases involving tech, or government actions? 
One sentence on what changed, one sentence on what it means.

End with two tasks for today:
(1) Pick one item from sections 1 or 2 and frame it as a question to analyze: 
    "Why would [company] do [this move] right now?"
(2) Name one AI or tech product from today's briefing I should spend 5 minutes trying.
```

The result: a briefing that arrives every morning, tailored to the company you're interviewing at, pre-loaded with your daily practice tasks. Configure it once, and it runs.

## Layer 2 — Active Synthesis

*Take one item from your passive intake and spend 10 minutes thinking through the strategic logic behind it.*

Each morning's briefing ends with one move to analyze. Take the question it surfaces — for example, *"Why would ChatGPT create a family product right now?"* — and work through the strategic logic using the same framework you'd use in a product strategy interview. You're **reverse-engineering a real company decision**.

### The five questions to ask

1. **What was the company's position before this move?** What competitive pressure or opportunity created the opening?
2. **Why now?** What changed in the market, the technology, or the regulatory environment?
3. **Who benefits and who is threatened?** Name specific companies or user segments.
4. **What are they trying to validate?** What does success look like for them 12 months from now?
5. **What would you have done differently?**

### Worked example — Why would ChatGPT create a family product?

> **Position before the move:** ChatGPT's user base skews toward power users, students, and professionals. Household penetration is high but unstructured. Parents with young children are anxious about uncontrolled AI access and often block it rather than managing it.
>
> **Why now:** Apple's Screen Time showed that parents will pay for structured controls. Competitors like Google (Gemini for Kids) and Amazon (Alexa parental controls) have already staked ground. OpenAI risks losing the household as a unit of account if it doesn't move.
>
> **Who benefits and who is threatened:** Families with school-age children benefit from a managed, safer AI experience. Google and Amazon are threatened on the household layer. Educational platforms like Khan Academy — built on top of GPT — could be squeezed if OpenAI moves into the homework-help lane directly.
>
> **What they're trying to validate:** Whether households will pay for a family-tier subscription rather than sharing a single adult account, and whether a safety-first positioning can unlock a segment that currently avoids AI tools for kids.
>
> **What you'd have done differently:** Lead the launch with educational positioning (homework help, reading comprehension) rather than safety positioning — the anxiety-driven framing attracts regulatory scrutiny before you've established trust.

That analysis took 10 minutes. Do it three times a week and after a month you'll have enough pattern recognition to hold a genuine strategic conversation about almost any company a product sense question throws at you.

### What a real interviewer said about this

> *"I probably overprepared for [Layer 1]. I came in with a thorough competitive breakdown, but the interviewer didn't seem to care much about the research itself. She quickly shifted to what I would do with it, how I would act on it, and how I would launch based on those insights."*
> — OpenAI growth PM candidate

**Awareness of the landscape is table stakes. Having a formed opinion on what to do next is what separates candidates.**

### How to sharpen this faster

Run this directly in Claude inside your prep project. Paste your briefing item and say:

> *"Walk me through the strategic logic behind this move using a product strategy framework. Then push back on my analysis when I share it."*

The back-and-forth sharpens your thinking faster than working through it alone.

### Meeting the Senior Bar

| Level | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| **Solid** | Reads newsletters, follows practitioners, has a working sense of what's happening. Builds background awareness. |
| **Senior+** | Has **written opinions** ahead of time — what the strategy is, why it might succeed or fail, what they'd do differently. The habit of writing opinions down (not just consuming others') produces answers that feel specific and defended rather than hedged and generic. Interviewers feel the difference immediately. They can tell when you're forming your opinion in real time vs. when you've thought through it prior. |

## Layer 3 — Hands-on Fluency

*Try at least one AI or tech product you're learning about — even briefly — so you have real opinions about it.*

Each morning's briefing ends with one product to try. **Try it. Five minutes is enough.**

The candidates who most convincingly discuss AI products in interviews are almost always the ones who use AI tools regularly for real tasks — not tutorials. They have actual opinions: things that surprised them, things that didn't work the way they expected, places where the product changed how they approached a problem. **You cannot get that from reading about a product. You get it from using it.**

### When you try a product, notice three things

1. **What does it do well?**
2. **Where does it feel rough or incomplete?**
3. **What would you change first if you were the PM?**

Those observations are exactly what interviewers at AI-native companies are listening for when they ask about product strategy or improvement questions.

Five minutes is not enough to become an expert. It is enough to have a genuine reaction — and that is what the interview is testing for.
