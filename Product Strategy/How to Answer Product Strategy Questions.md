# How to Answer Product Strategy Questions

> Product strategy questions are the hardest interview questions to do well, and the easiest to do adequately. A structured, coherent answer is not the bar. The bar is a structured answer with a point of view that the interviewer wouldn't have heard from the last five candidates.

---

## Why This Matters Now

The AI era has made product strategy questions harder in a specific way: **the landscape changes faster than frameworks can keep up with.** What was a defensible competitive position 18 months ago may be a commodity today. Candidates who walk in with a static view of a company's strategy — formed from a case study or a LinkedIn post — get exposed quickly.

Interviewers at Meta, Stripe, and OpenAI are now asking strategy questions specifically to see if you can **reason about a dynamic market**, not just describe the current one.

AI has also changed what interviewers listen for. It's now easy to generate a strategy answer that sounds polished but has no real thinking behind it. Interviewers know this. A clean structure alone no longer impresses them. What they're screening for now is a **genuine, well-reasoned point of view** underneath that structure — one that shows the candidate actually thought through the tradeoffs rather than reciting a template.

---

## What Interviewers Are Looking For

**A point of view, not a summary.**
Can you take a position? The candidate who says *"here's the strongest option"* and defends it is more memorable than the one who says *"here are three balanced options."* Interviewers want to hire PMs who can make calls, not produce memos.

**Business model fluency.**
Do you know what this company actually optimizes for — not just what its mission says? A candidate who treats Meta's strategy question as being about social connection and not advertising revenue is reasoning about the wrong company.

**Landscape awareness.**
Do you know what's actually happening in the market right now? Not generic trend talk, but specific shifts that affect this company's position. Candidates who've done the homework are immediately distinct from candidates who've done the framework.

**Principle-driven option filtering.**
Can you rule out options for specific reasons, not just pick the one that sounds good? The best strategy answers generate a realistic option set and then eliminate most of it based on constraints that are specific to this company at this moment.

**A defensible close.**
Can you make a call, name the strongest counter-argument, and explain why you still land where you land? This is the move that separates senior candidates from solid junior ones.

## The 6-Step Framework

> The structure is the container for the work. Two candidates can follow the same six steps and produce answers that feel completely different — because one has an opinion and one has a template.

**Running example used throughout:** *"What is the biggest threat to Reddit's business?"*

---

### Step 1 — Clarify the Question

Strategy questions are often deliberately underspecified. The interviewer wants to see how you scope.

**Before you start, establish:**
- What time horizon is in scope: a 6-month decision or a 3-year strategy?
- What does success look like, how is it measured, and who is it measured by?
- Are there constraints to name upfront: budget, regulatory, existing product bets?

Two or three clarifying questions signal maturity. The right questions are about **scope and success definition**, not features.

> **Reddit example:** Clarifying questions worth asking are:
> - *Time horizon* — near-term threats (short-form video eating engagement) look completely different from long-term ones (AI commoditizing community discussion)
> - *Definition of business* — the biggest threat to Reddit's ad revenue and the biggest threat to their data licensing business are different answers
> - *Breadth vs. depth* — some interviewers want a full landscape before you prioritize; others want a single thesis defended hard

**You don't need all answers before proceeding.** State your assumptions:

> *"I'll assume this is a 12-month horizon, and that the primary success metric is revenue impact rather than engagement."*

That's a legitimate opening that immediately demonstrates strategic framing.

> **Tip:** The best clarifying questions reveal a strategic hypothesis. *"Is the goal defending the core business or exploring adjacencies? Because those suggest very different option sets"* tells the interviewer you already have an opinion forming. That's the difference between asking to understand and asking to frame.

---

### Step 2 — Anchor to Business Goals

This is the step most candidates get wrong — they anchor to what a company **says** it cares about rather than what it **actually optimizes for**. Mission statements and 10-Ks are not the same document.

**For any strategy question, establish:**
- What are the company's actual revenue streams? Where does the money come from?
- What does growth look like for this business at this stage?
- What is the company's revealed preference: where are they actually investing?

> **Reddit example:** Reddit has two primary revenue streams — advertising and data licensing, including API access. A candidate who treats Reddit as a community platform optimizing purely for engagement is reasoning about a less complete version of the company. The candidate who observes that advertising CPMs on Reddit are lower than on Meta and YouTube, and that data licensing represents a growing share of revenue that depends on the open-web ecosystem, is reasoning about the actual business.

### Meeting the Senior Bar

| Level | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| **Solid** | Accurately captures the company's stated mission and uses it as a stable anchor. *"Meta's goal is to connect people and grow engagement"* — correct and serviceable. |
| **Senior+** | Surfaces the **tension** between what a company says and how it actually makes money. *"Meta's stated goal is connection, but its business model runs on advertising revenue — and those two things pull in different directions once AI enters the picture. AI-generated content can drive engagement metrics while quietly degrading the social signal that makes Meta's ad targeting valuable."* Naming that tension unprompted, and letting it inform which strategic options are worth pursuing, is the differentiator. |

---

### Step 3 — Map the Landscape

This step establishes what's actually happening in the market that makes this question worth asking **now**. A strategy question always has a triggering condition: something changed, or something is about to.

**Three things to define:**

**1. What is the threat or opportunity, and where does it come from?**
Not *"AI is disrupting everything"* — specifically what is happening and to whom. Google's AI Overviews does not threaten all publishers equally. It threatens publishers who rank for informational queries and monetize through traffic. Your landscape analysis has to be that specific.

**2. What are competitors already doing?**
Not a feature comparison, but a **strategic posture** comparison. Are they going broad or deep? Are they defending or attacking? The question isn't *"what features do they have"* but *"what is their theory of how they win."*

**3. What is the timing signal?**
Why is this a question now? What changed — a technology unlock, a market shift, a regulatory change, a competitor move? If you can't answer *why now*, you're not ready to answer the strategy question.

> **Reddit example:** The landscape map must include:
> - The rise of AI-generated content degrading discussion quality on open platforms
> - LLMs trained on Reddit data creating alternatives that don't require the community infrastructure Reddit has built
> - The structural shift in search from blue-link results (which sent traffic to Reddit) toward AI summaries (which extract the value without the referral)
>
> That's a specific, textured landscape — not a generic competitive analysis.

---

### Step 4 — Set Guiding Principles

This is the bridge between your analysis and your decision — and candidates often skip it entirely. That's why their Step 6 decisions feel disconnected from their Steps 2 and 3 work.

**Guiding principles are not values. They are constraints. A good guiding principle eliminates options.**

If your principle doesn't rule anything out, it isn't doing any work.

**How to set principles:**
1. Take your most important Step 2 observation (what the business actually optimizes for) → ask: *what does that rule out?* → that's your first principle.
2. Take your most important Step 3 observation (what's happening and why now) → ask: *what category of response does that make irrelevant or actively harmful?* → that's your second principle.
3. Identify the single biggest tension in your analysis → turn it into a tiebreaker. → that's your third principle.

> **Reddit example — principles that do work:**
> 1. *"We will not pursue any strategy that requires alienating the existing moderator community, because the moderation infrastructure is what differentiates Reddit's content quality from generic UGC platforms."* → Kills any strategy that monetizes moderator labor directly.
> 2. *"We will not compete on ad targeting precision with Meta; our comparative advantage is contextual relevance, not behavioral data."* → Eliminates chasing Meta's advertising product roadmap.
> 3. *"We will prioritize strategies that compound — that build moats over time rather than just driving near-term revenue."* → Eliminates one-time plays like a content licensing deal that doesn't create a durable advantage.

### Meeting the Senior Bar

| Level | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| **Solid** | Principles are real and clearly stated. *"We should prioritize user trust"* lands fine — signals strategic awareness. |
| **Senior+** | Principles are specific enough to **close doors**. *"We won't pursue any strategy that requires Reddit to behave more like a social network and less like a community platform, because the users who create the most value have self-selected away from social networks for exactly that reason."* — that principle rules something out. **Useful test:** if a guiding principle doesn't eliminate at least one option on the table, it's probably not constraining enough to be worth stating. |

---

### Step 5 — Generate and Filter Options

This is the **only generative step** in the framework. Every other step is analytical. Here, you actually produce the option set.

**Two-stage process:**

**Generate first.** Give yourself 60–90 seconds to brainstorm without filtering. Write down more than you plan to use. The goal is to avoid anchoring on the first option you think of — which is usually the most obvious one and rarely the most interesting.

**Then filter by your principles.** Take each option and check it against Step 4. Options that violate your principles are eliminated. The ones that survive are your shortlist.

> **Reddit example:** Options might include — doubling down on data licensing; building a native AI assistant trained on Reddit data; creating a premium subscription for power users; expanding internationally; or acquiring content moderation technology.
>
> Filter through the principles: any option that commoditizes the community or requires chasing Meta's ad tech gets cut. **Shortlist: data licensing play, native AI assistant, premium subscription.**

> **Watch out for:** Presenting more than three options in the final shortlist. More than three signals the filtering step didn't work. An interviewer who hears *"here are seven options to consider"* knows the candidate hasn't done the work of deciding what actually matters.

---

### Step 6 — Argue and Close

This is where most candidates underperform — they spend the answer getting to this point and then stop short of the actual work: **making a call and defending it against the strongest counter-argument.**

**Two things this step requires:**

**1. Make a specific recommendation.**
Not *"it depends"* and not *"I'd prioritize a combination of approaches."* Which option, why, and over what time horizon? If you can't say it in two sentences, the recommendation isn't sharp enough.

**2. Name and defeat the counter-argument.**
The senior move is not to pick the right option. It's to pick an option, name the strongest case for the alternative, and explain why you still land where you land. That's what separates a decision from a preference.

> **Reddit example:**
>
> **Recommendation:** Invest in the native AI assistant strategy, using Reddit's proprietary corpus as a training and grounding advantage.
>
> **Strongest counter-argument:** Capital-intensive, requires ML capabilities Reddit doesn't have at scale, and risks cannibalizing the traffic-based discovery behavior that drives ad revenue.
>
> **Why to proceed anyway:** The data licensing business is already under pressure as LLM providers internalize training data. Waiting to build an AI product until that business erodes means competing from a weaker position. The time to invest is before the leverage disappears, not after.
>
> **What to watch:** *"The signal that I'm wrong is if API licensing revenue holds or grows through the next 12 months — which would mean the external LLM ecosystem still needs Reddit's data. I'd revisit the build decision at that point."*
>
> That's not hedging. That's showing you know the difference between a decision and a certainty.

### Meeting the Senior Bar

| Level | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| **Solid** | Makes a clear recommendation and explains the case for it. *"I'd prioritize the AI assistant strategy because Reddit's data corpus is a real moat and the timing is right."* — defensible and clean. |
| **Senior+** | Argues against the alternatives directly. *"I'd prioritize the AI assistant over the premium subscription play, even though subscription has better near-term unit economics. Subscription captures value from existing high-intent users but doesn't change Reddit's structural position — it doesn't create a new moat. The AI product is harder to execute but it's the only option on this list that turns Reddit's community data into a compounding advantage rather than a depleting one."* The senior move is **naming the strongest counter-argument and defeating it on the merits.** |

## Common Pitfalls

**Treating the framework as the answer.**
A candidate who moves through six steps cleanly but never takes a position has produced a consulting slide deck, not a strategy recommendation. Structure is the container. The opinion is the content.

**Anchoring on the mission statement.**
*"Meta's goal is to connect the world"* is not a business analysis. Every company's stated goal is noble. The strategy question is about what they're actually optimizing for — revealed in revenue streams, capital allocation, and product bets, not taglines.

**Generic landscape analysis.**
*"AI is disrupting everything"* is not a landscape. *"AI Overviews reduces organic search traffic for informational queries, which specifically hurts Reddit's SEO-driven discovery, which is the top-of-funnel for user acquisition"* is a landscape. Specificity is not showing off. It's the actual work.

**Principles that don't constrain.**
*"We should prioritize user trust"* and *"we should be innovative"* are not guiding principles. They are aspirations. Principles that don't eliminate options don't help you filter the option set.

**Stopping before the counter-argument.**
A recommendation without a counter-argument is a preference. Interviewers will probe. Build the probe into your answer before they ask it.

**Generic AI-assisted answers.**
Balanced, structured answers that consider multiple perspectives are everywhere now. An answer that says *"on the one hand... on the other hand... in conclusion, it depends on the company's priorities"* will not be remembered. A clear point of view — even an imperfect one — is more valuable than a hedge.

---

## Framework at a Glance

| Step | Action | Key Question to Answer |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Clarify** | Scope the question | What time horizon, success metric, and constraints apply? |
| **2. Business Goals** | Anchor to actual revenue drivers | What does this company actually optimize for? |
| **3. Landscape** | Map the market right now | What changed, and why is this question being asked today? |
| **4. Principles** | Set constraints that eliminate options | What rules out at least one option on the table? |
| **5. Options** | Generate, then filter | What survives the principles? (Keep the shortlist to ≤3) |
| **6. Close** | Recommend and defeat the counter | Which option, and why doesn't the strongest alternative win? |
