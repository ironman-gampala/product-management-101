# Rubric for Product Strategy Interviews

> Built from real rubrics used inside big tech companies and AI frontier labs. Interviewers score five dimensions on a four-point scale.

**The four-point scale:** Strong No Hire → No Hire → Hire → Strong Hire

All five dimensions carry **equal weight**. A candidate who aces three and falls flat on two is not a hire. The rubric is designed to catch the full picture, not just reward structured thinking.

---

## Before You Read the Dimensions

The bar has shifted. AI tools have made it trivially easy to produce a well-structured, balanced, plausible-sounding strategy answer. Interviewers at Perplexity, Stripe, and OpenAI have recalibrated in response. They've raised the floor on what *"good"* looks like — and gotten much better at detecting an answer that was **assembled** rather than **thought through**.

Four of the five dimensions below have always mattered. The fifth — **Genuine POV** — is rapidly becoming the one that separates candidates at the margin, because it's the only dimension that can't be AI-ified.

---

## The Five Dimensions

---

### 1. Business Model Fluency

Every strategy answer lives or dies on this foundation. If you're reasoning about the wrong company, everything downstream is wrong — even if the logic is clean.

There's almost always a gap between a company's stated mission and its revealed preferences. Meta says it wants to connect people. But Meta's business is advertising revenue — and those two things create real tension the moment any strategy question touches AI features or engagement design. **A candidate who names that tension unprompted has demonstrated something no framework can produce: they've actually thought about this company.**

> **What interviewers notice immediately:** A candidate who anchors their strategy to the mission statement sounds like they read the About page. A candidate who anchors it to the revenue model sounds like they've been paying attention.
>
> At Stripe, strategy questions consistently probe whether candidates understand the cost structure of payments infrastructure — not just the developer-friendly positioning. If you're treating Stripe as a developer tools company and ignoring the financial infrastructure layer, you're reasoning about the wrong business.

| Score | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| **Strong No Hire** | Strategy is entirely disconnected from the business model. Candidate treats mission statements as business strategy. |
| **No Hire** | Surface-level read on the business. Knows what the company does, but misses how it actually makes money or what it's optimizing for right now. |
| **Hire** | Accurately identifies the company's revenue model and primary growth drivers. Strategy aligns with what the business actually needs. |
| **Strong Hire** | Surfaces the tension between stated goals and actual company behavior, and uses that tension to filter options. Knows where the company is in its growth arc and what that means for the decision at hand. The recommendation reflects a real read on the business, not just a correct one. |

---

### 2. Landscape Accuracy

Landscape analysis is where most candidates sound the same. *"AI is disrupting this space"* or *"there are several strong competitors"* are sentences any candidate could generate without knowing anything about the specific company or moment.

What separates a strong landscape read: **specificity and timing.** Not what the market looks like in general, but what changed recently and why that makes this question urgent *now*. Strategy questions always have a triggering condition. The candidate who can name it demonstrates they've been paying attention — not just studying.

**A common trap:** Candidates treat competitive analysis as a feature comparison. *"Competitor A has X, Competitor B has Y"* is a product catalog, not a competitive analysis. What interviewers want is a **strategic posture read**: what does each competitor's move reveal about how they believe they win?

> **The distinction that matters:** Distinguish between who the competitor is and what threat model they represent.
> - **Google entering a space** = a distribution threat
> - **An AI-native startup entering the same space** = a product architecture threat
>
> Those require completely different strategic responses. Candidates who collapse them into *"increased competition"* have done half the analysis.

| Score | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| **Strong No Hire** | Little to no awareness of the competitive or market landscape. Analysis could apply to any company in any industry. |
| **No Hire** | Names the right players, but analysis stays at the surface. No sense of timing, posture, or what specifically has changed. |
| **Hire** | Accurate, specific competitive analysis. Names real players, describes their strategic positioning, and identifies the market shift that makes this question relevant now. |
| **Strong Hire** | Goes beyond *who* the competitors are to *how* the competitive dynamics are shifting. Names the timing signal, explains why now is different from 18 months ago, and connects AI-driven market shifts to specific strategic implications for this company. The interviewer learns something from the landscape section. |

### 3. Decision Quality

This is the dimension that most directly tests whether you can do the job. PMs get paid to make decisions with incomplete information and defend them to skeptical stakeholders. A strategy interview is a simulation of exactly that.

**The failure mode here is not giving a wrong answer. It's giving no answer.** Candidates who arrive at the recommendation step and say *"it depends on the team's priorities"* have told the interviewer they don't have a point of view.

The senior move: name the strongest case for the alternative before anyone asks, then explain why you still land where you land. That move shows you've actually considered the alternatives rather than picked the first reasonable option. **If you wait for the interviewer to surface the counter-argument, you've already lost ground.**

> **What separates a decision from a preference:**
>
> *Preference:* "I'd go with option A because it has better unit economics."
>
> *Decision:* "I'd go with option A over option B even though option B has better near-term unit economics, because option A is the only one that changes our structural position. Safer options that don't move the needle aren't actually safe — they're just slow losses."
>
> The second version has a counter-argument built in. The first one doesn't survive the first follow-up question.

| Score | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| **Strong No Hire** | No recommendation made. Answer ends with a list of options or an *"it depends"* hedge. |
| **No Hire** | Makes a recommendation, but it's too generic to be defensible — or abandons it immediately when questioned. |
| **Hire** | Makes a clear, specific recommendation and explains the reasoning. Can defend it when pushed. |
| **Strong Hire** | Makes a call, names the strongest counter-argument without being prompted, and explains why they still land where they do. Names the signal that would cause them to change course. The recommendation is specific enough that it could be wrong — and the candidate knows it. |

### 4. Structural Clarity

A clear answer isn't just one that's easy to listen to. It's one where the reasoning **builds**: what you say about the business informs what you say about the landscape, which in turn explains why you ruled out certain options, which naturally leads to your recommendation.

Interviewers are scoring whether your thinking holds together as a chain — not just whether each individual point sounds reasonable.

**The most common failure here isn't being disorganized.** It's producing good individual observations that never connect. You can have a sharp read on the business model, accurate landscape analysis, and a clear recommendation — and still score No Hire on this dimension if those three things feel like separate answers to separate questions.

> **The connecting sentence most candidates leave implicit:**
>
> Before your recommendation, say out loud why it follows from what you established earlier:
>
> *"Because the core threat is to Reddit's search-driven acquisition funnel, and any response that alienates the moderator community makes that worse, the only option that survives is..."*
>
> That one sentence does the work. Interviewers notice when a candidate draws a conclusion rather than just states one.

| Score | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| **Strong No Hire** | Answer is hard to follow. Reasoning is circular, incomplete, or jumps around without clear logic. |
| **No Hire** | Some structure present but the reasoning has gaps. Steps feel independent rather than building on each other. |
| **Hire** | Clear, logical structure throughout. Each step follows from the previous one. Recommendation feels earned. |
| **Strong Hire** | Structure is invisible because it serves the thinking so well. The recommendation connects explicitly back to earlier analysis. The candidate signals where they are in their answer and why each section matters. Easy to follow, hard to poke holes in. |

### 5. Genuine POV

This is the dimension most candidates don't realize is being scored. And right now, **it's the dimension that matters most at the margin.**

Here's why: four of the five dimensions on this rubric can be improved through practice. You can get better at business model fluency by studying revenue models. You can improve landscape accuracy by following the industry. You can sharpen decision quality by practicing the counter-argument move. You can clean up structural clarity by recording yourself and listening back.

**Genuine POV can't be trained in.** It either comes from real engagement with the space or it doesn't.

**What it looks like when it's there:** The candidate says something the interviewer didn't expect. They name a tension the framework wouldn't surface. They have an actual opinion on where a company is making a mistake. They've used the product and noticed something specific. It's the sentence that makes the interviewer put down their pen.

**What it looks like when it's absent:** A perfectly structured answer that could have been given by any reasonably prepared candidate with a good AI assistant. Nothing surprising. Nothing earned. A recommendation that's defensible but forgettable.

> **The test to run on your own answers:** Could five other candidates have given this exact answer? If yes, Genuine POV scores No Hire — regardless of how clean the structure was.
>
> The candidates who move to Strong Hire on this dimension are the ones who bring something the interviewer couldn't have generated from a prompt. That's a high bar. It's also the most durable advantage you can build, because it compounds the more you engage with the industry.

| Score | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| **Strong No Hire** | Answer feels generated. No distinct perspective anywhere. Could have been produced by any candidate with a framework and decent preparation. |
| **No Hire** | Has some opinions but they feel performed. Nothing unexpected. The candidate is executing the interview rather than thinking through the problem. |
| **Hire** | Clear point of view that feels earned. The recommendation reflects real thinking about this specific company and moment — not just framework application. |
| **Strong Hire** | Says something the interviewer didn't expect. Names a tension or opportunity the framework wouldn't surface. It's obvious this candidate has been paying attention to this industry for reasons that have nothing to do with this interview. The interviewer remembers what they said. |

### What Genuine POV Looks Like at AI-Native Companies

| Company | What They're Listening For |
|---|---|
| **Anthropic** | Candidates who care about AI safety as a real problem — not a talking point |
| **OpenAI** | Strategy answers that connect to the AGI mission; answers that don't feel thin |
| **Common thread** | These companies want people who are genuinely in the conversation — not people who studied for the interview |

## At-a-Glance Scoring Summary

| Dimension | Strong No Hire | No Hire | Hire | Strong Hire |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Business Model Fluency** | Disconnected from revenue model | Surface-level; misses what company optimizes for | Accurately identifies revenue model and growth drivers | Names mission-vs-reality tension; uses it to filter options |
| **Landscape Accuracy** | Generic; applies to any company | Names players but misses timing and posture | Specific players, positioning, and market shift | Explains *how* dynamics are shifting; interviewer learns something |
| **Decision Quality** | No recommendation; ends in "it depends" | Recommendation too generic; collapses under questioning | Clear recommendation; can defend when pushed | Proactively names counter-argument; names the signal to watch |
| **Structural Clarity** | Hard to follow; circular or jumpy | Gaps between steps; observations feel disconnected | Each step follows the last; recommendation feels earned | Connecting tissue is explicit; impossible to poke holes in |
| **Genuine POV** | Feels generated; no distinct perspective | Opinions feel performed; nothing unexpected | Point of view feels earned; specific to this company | Says something the interviewer didn't expect; memorable |
