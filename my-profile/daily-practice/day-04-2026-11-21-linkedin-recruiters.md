## What went well
- **First valid guardrail in any mock:** the share of candidates marking recruiter InMails as spam. It could really get worse while hires go up.
- **First "To summarise…" in any mock.** It was messy, but it was there.
- **North star landed on an outcome (hires), and I read it back at the end.** That's better than Day 3's usage metrics.
- **"Only LinkedIn" signals:** open to a move, same title for years (due for a step up), pay band fit, past InMail replies.
- **Built on what exists:** I improved the existing match feature and reused the "hide from my employer" setting for privacy.
- **Root cause when probed:** "reaching out to the whole ocean" instead of a targeted set. Low reply rate is the symptom.
- **Honest pay-data answer:** title plus salary data plus a partner like Levels.fyi, and I said it's an estimate.
- Called the product a two-sided marketplace (recruiter and candidate) early.
- Hidden pay band in the JD is a nice detail.

## What to improve
1. **Decision first, reasons after.** Every time the interviewer asked for one line, I gave a paragraph.
2. **Asking for time: 3 times** (clarify, users, guardrail), plus "1 second". Worse than Day 3.
3. **Only one solution, straight into detail**, even with the 3-one-liner instruction in front of me. I never answered "what else did you consider?".
4. **Problem statement with "because": still never said**, even when the interviewer asked for it directly. Fourth mock running.
5. **Mixing steps came back:** I walked the journey and pains inside the users step.

## How it went

I opened by asking for a minute to think, then asked one real question: "why recruiters?". That was a good
question and got me the why-now (Easy Apply flood, falling InMail replies), but I didn't scope the product or
the recruiter type. In strategy I used the why-now well and made a sound "mature product, so retention" call.
My north star then changed mid-sentence, from "candidates sourced" to "candidates converted", and I never
defined "converted" or wrote it on the board. The interviewer had to do it for me.

At users I asked for "a bunch of minutes" to plan users, pains and problem statements together. After a push
I talked, but walked the journey and pains instead of segmenting. The interviewer had to ask "which
recruiter?". Then I picked mid-size companies (200–2,000 people) hiring experienced people, and rejected
campus hiring.

In pain points I found a real insight: the recruiter can't see whether someone is open to a move, fits the
salary range, or is happy where they are. I didn't number these as three pains, and I didn't give a problem
statement. When the interviewer asked "symptom or problem?", I found the root cause: "the whole ocean".

In solutions I went straight into one idea (an AI matchmaker with a weighted score), with good only-LinkedIn
signals. I never named alternatives, even when asked. The flow covered 5 of the 6 steps, with no edge case.
The close was my best yet: an honest pay-data answer, v1 ordering, a metrics funnel (reply → interview →
hire), a real guardrail (spam reports), a moonshot (candidate-side "why this fits you" insight), and a first
"To summarise…". The summary mixed in Easy Apply noise, which wasn't the problem I picked, and it had no v1
or guardrail.

**Big insight: I explain before I decide.** Each time I was asked for a one-line decision (north star, which
recruiter, the problem, what else), I gave reasoning first, and the decision came late, came vague, or didn't
come. The interviewer had to pull me four times. The fix is to say the decision sentence first and the
reasons after.

## Model turn: pain points + problem statement (about 60 seconds)

> *"Let me start with their goal. A recruiter at a 500-person company wants to fill a senior role with a strong
> hire in about a month.*
>
> *Their journey goes: the role opens, they search, they build a shortlist, they send InMails, people reply,
> interviews, offer. I see three pains along it.*
>
> *One, at search: the filters show title and years of experience, but not whether someone actually wants to
> move.*
>
> *Two, at outreach: they send 50 InMails and get maybe 5 replies, because they're messaging people who are
> happy where they are, or who are too expensive.*
>
> *Three, at offer: good candidates drop out late over salary.*
>
> *I'll pick the outreach pain. It's the biggest drop in the funnel, it happens on every role, and it's exactly
> the why-now: falling reply rates. Search is really the cause of it, and offer drop-off happens less often.*
>
> *[Writes on board: Mid-size company recruiters struggle to get replies from experienced candidates **because**
> they're reaching out to the whole ocean with no signal of who is open to a move or fits their pay range,
> **which leads to** hours of wasted outreach and roles staying open for months.]"*

| Move | Why it scores |
|---|---|
| Goal with a number ("a month") | Gives the pains something to be measured against |
| Pains from 3 different steps | Makes the pick a real choice |
| Numbered "one, two, three" | Sounds structured; the interviewer can follow |
| Pick with 2 reasons plus why not the others | Rule #1: decision, reason, reject |
| Problem statement with "because" and "which leads to" | The line I haven't said in four mocks |

## Fixed summary (what I could have said)

> *"To summarise: for recruiters at mid-size companies hiring experienced people, the real problem is low
> InMail replies, because they reach out to the whole ocean with no signal of who's open or fits their pay.
> I'd build a likely-to-reply ranking in Recruiter, starting with Open to Work, title tenure and past reply
> rate, with pay fit added once the estimates are good enough. I'd know it works if the share of
> LinkedIn-sourced candidates hired within 45 days goes up, while the spam-report rate on InMails doesn't.
> Next, the candidate side: telling them why a role fits them."*
