# Rubric for Product Sense Interviews

> Built from real rubrics used inside big tech companies and AI frontier labs. Product sense interviews are scored on five dimensions, each on a four-point scale.

**The four-point scale:** Strong No Hire → No Hire → Hire → Strong Hire

---

## Before You Read the Dimensions

**Prototyping ability is the fastest-growing differentiator at the margin.** AI tools have made it easy to generate a structured product answer in seconds — which means structured is now the floor, not the ceiling.

What separates candidates increasingly is whether they can translate a product concept into something real, narrate tradeoffs live, and handle implementation follow-ups. This skill can be practiced. Candidates who have practiced it have a visible advantage.

---

## The Five Dimensions

---

### 1. Business Acumen

This dimension scores whether you understand **why a company would build this product** — not just what users might want.

A strong answer:
- Connects your product direction to the company's mission and business model *before* the interviewer asks
- Names the competitive gap
- Explains why this opportunity exists right now

> **One sentence on timing is worth three on market size.**

At AI-native companies like OpenAI, Anthropic, and Perplexity, interviewers probe for genuine fluency with how AI capabilities and constraints shape the design space.

| Response | Score |
|---|---|
| *"We could use AI to personalize it"* | No Hire |
| Names what the technology can and cannot do yet, connects that to the product framing | Strong Hire |

| Score | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| **Strong No Hire** | No meaningful connection to the company's mission or market position. Could be an answer for any company on any prompt. |
| **No Hire** | Mentions the mission but doesn't connect product decisions to it. Strategy feels like a checkbox. |
| **Hire** | Grounds product direction in the company's mission and names a relevant competitive or timing dynamic. The answer could not be given for a different company without significant rework. |
| **Strong Hire** | Explains why *this company*, with its specific capabilities and position, is the right team to build *this* right now. The interviewer learns something from the strategy section. |

---

### 2. User-Centricity

This dimension scores whether you genuinely think from a **real user's perspective**, or generate plausible-sounding users and pain points that could apply to any product.

The difference shows up in three places:

| Area | Generic (No Hire) | Specific (Hire+) |
|---|---|---|
| **Segmentation** | Segment could belong to any product | Segment could only belong to *this* product |
| **Pain points** | Feel pre-loaded; plausible for anyone | Surface from the user's actual day; come from different root-cause buckets |
| **Prioritization** | Picks the obvious choice without criteria | Named explicit criteria across more than one dimension |

> **Note:** The candidates who struggle most in pain points are almost always the ones who rushed segmentation. When the segment is generic, the problems are generic. They follow each other.

| Score | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| **Strong No Hire** | User types could apply to any product. Pain points feel pre-loaded. No connection to what this technology specifically enables. |
| **No Hire** | Segment is reasonable but generic. Pain points are plausible but not specific to this user or product. Prioritization picks the obvious choice without criteria. |
| **Hire** | Segment is specific to this technology or product. Pain points surface from the user's actual experience and come from different root-cause buckets. Prioritization is reasoned across more than one dimension. |
| **Strong Hire** | The segment could only belong to this product. Pain points feel discovered, not retrieved. Prioritization connects to company mission, technology constraints, and signal quality. The interviewer could not have predicted the call — and agrees with it. |

---

### 3. Product Taste

This dimension scores whether your ideas are **meaningfully different from each other**, grounded in the root problem, and whether at least one shows a longer product arc.

**The fast test for distinctness:** Each idea should be able to fail independently. If two would only fail together, they are the same idea.

**Taste also shows in conviction.** Interviewers want to see at least one idea you're genuinely excited about, and a point of view on what is worth building versus what should wait.

> Candidates who deprioritize precisely — naming what would need to be true for the other ideas to become the right call — score meaningfully higher than candidates who just pick the safest option.
>
> *"Too complex"* is not a reason for punting. *"Requires behavioral modeling the technology cannot yet support"* is.

| Score | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| **Strong No Hire** | Ideas are variations of the same concept. No connection to the root problem. No product arc visible. |
| **No Hire** | Ideas are distinct enough to compare but feel generic. Prioritization is one-dimensional. No moonshot or longer vision. |
| **Hire** | Three genuinely distinct ideas, each grounded in the root problem. Prioritization connects to the north star metric. At least one idea shows product vision. |
| **Strong Hire** | At least one idea surprises the interviewer. Deprioritization is argued precisely. The answer would be materially different if a different company had given the same prompt. |

---

### 4. Prototyping Ability

This dimension scores whether you can **translate a product concept into something visual and concrete**, narrate your design decisions as you go, and treat the prototype as a prop rather than a deliverable.

Interviewers are **not** scoring visual polish. They are scoring:
- Whether the screens you show connect directly to the user and problem you identified
- Whether you can explain every choice you made

Some interviews ask you to sketch on a whiteboard. Others give you a tool like Claude Design or ask you to build live (or bring your own). Either way: **show the one or two screens that carry the most product value, make your design decisions explicit, and walk the interviewer through what the experience actually feels like for the user.**

| Score | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| **Strong No Hire** | Unable to build anything functional, or the prototype is disconnected from the earlier design work. |
| **No Hire** | Gets something on screen but without a clear prompt strategy or design rationale. Struggles when asked about implementation tradeoffs. |
| **Hire** | Produces a functional prototype with a structured prompt grounded in the product sense work. Narrates design decisions and handles follow-up questions with composure. |
| **Strong Hire** | Brings the interviewer into every decision: why these screens, what the empty states look like, what happens when the product fails. Proactively addresses production-readiness. The narration would be valuable even without the prototype. |

---

### 5. Communication & Collaboration

This dimension scores whether the interviewer always knows **where you are and why**, and whether you treat the interview as a conversation rather than a performance.

- **Communication** shows up in transitions
- **Collaboration** shows up in whether you check in at key decisions, invite pushback, and adjust when you get new information

**The end of the main answer is not the end of the interview.** At Meta, the product sense round almost always shifts into a conflicting-metric scenario. At AI-native companies, expect technical implementation probes. Handling those with composure and connecting back to earlier reasoning is part of the score.

| Score | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| **Strong No Hire** | Interviewer struggles to follow the answer. No transitions. Does not engage with follow-up questions. |
| **No Hire** | Answer is followable but requires effort. Transitions are missing. Candidate presents rather than discusses. |
| **Hire** | Clear transitions at each step. Handles follow-up questions with composure and connects back to earlier reasoning. |
| **Strong Hire** | Treats the interview as a collaborative exercise throughout. Handles ethics probes, conflicting-metric questions, and prototyping follow-ups as natural extensions of the answer. The interviewer feels like a participant, not an audience. |

---

## At-a-Glance Scoring Summary

| Dimension | Strong No Hire | No Hire | Hire | Strong Hire |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Business Acumen** | No connection to mission or market | Mission mentioned but not applied | Grounded in mission; names timing dynamic | Explains why *this* company, *right now*; interviewer learns something |
| **User-Centricity** | Generic users; pre-loaded pain points | Reasonable but generic; obvious prioritization | Specific segment; pains from different buckets; multi-criteria prioritization | Segment belongs only to this product; pains feel discovered; interviewer agrees with the call |
| **Product Taste** | Variations of one idea; no product arc | Distinct but generic; no moonshot | Three distinct ideas; metric-connected prioritization; shows vision | Interviewer surprised; deprioritization argued precisely; company-specific answer |
| **Prototyping Ability** | Nothing functional or disconnected | Something on screen; no rationale; struggles with tradeoffs | Functional prototype; narrates decisions; handles follow-ups | Every decision explained; production-readiness addressed; narration alone would be valuable |
| **Communication** | Hard to follow; no transitions; no engagement | Followable with effort; presents rather than discusses | Clear transitions; composure under follow-ups; connects back to reasoning | Interview feels collaborative; handles probes as natural extensions; interviewer is a participant |
