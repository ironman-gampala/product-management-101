# Deep Dive: Brainstorming Product Ideas

> Generating ideas is the easy part. Generating ideas that are **meaningfully different from each other** and that connect back to the specific root problem you identified is what separates a product thinker from someone who can fill a list. This step is where product taste shows — and interviewers immediately feel its presence or absence.

---

## What Interviewers Are Looking For

| Signal | What It Means |
|---|---|
| **Genuine distinctiveness** | Are your ideas different from each other, or variations on the same approach? |
| **Root problem connection** | Does each idea connect back to the root problem you identified — not to a different problem? |
| **Product arc** | Do you have at least one idea that shows a longer-term vision? |
| **Metric alignment** | Is your prioritization rationale connected to the north star metric and mission from Step 2? |
| **Conviction** | Do you show genuine excitement about one direction — not just dutiful options? |

---

## The Product Cut Framework

Most candidates brainstorm within a single dimension — three features, or three UX variations. Because all the ideas fall into the same category, they end up with redundant options that collapse into a single choice, and the interviewer never sees the candidate's range.

**Most candidates generate features rather than solutions.**
*"A notification when the dog is distressed"* is a feature. *"A real-time emotional signal that closes the owner's feedback loop"* is a solution. Features are implementations of solutions. You need the solution direction first — otherwise, the features lack coherence.

**Product cuts solve this.** A cut is a technology or delivery lens you apply to a problem. Each lens produces a different class of idea.

### The Five Cuts — Build Your Shortlist

| Cut | What It Produces | Example Direction |
|---|---|---|
| **AI & Software** | A new information layer, intelligence, or personalization | Real-time signal, pattern detection, personalized recommendations |
| **Hardware / Physical layer** | A wearable, sensor, or physical interaction point | Device that captures data the software can't |
| **Platform / Network** | Social, marketplace, or ecosystem effects | Community loop, shared data, third-party integrations |
| **Ambient / Environmental** | Passive, background, or contextual experience | Something the user doesn't actively interact with |
| **Moonshot** | A longer-arc vision that requires future technology or scale | What this becomes in 3 years if constraints are lifted |

> You don't need to apply every cut to every problem. You need enough to generate **genuinely distinct ideas** — typically three.

## Step 1 — Anchor to the Root Problem

Before generating a single idea, **state the root problem in one sentence.**

Not the pain point. The root problem underneath it.

| ❌ Pain Point | ✓ Root Problem |
|---|---|
| *"Owners are anxious about their dog while they are away"* | *"Owners have no feedback loop, so every care decision is made in the dark"* |

Your solutions must address the root problem. If they only address the surface-level symptom, they will feel thin when the interviewer probes further.

> **Note:** The candidates who generate the best solutions are the ones who took the pain point step seriously. If you named a specific root cause (not just a frustration), this step almost writes itself. If you find yourself struggling to generate distinct ideas, the problem is usually that you never got past the symptom.

**For the OpenAI animal mind-reading prompt:**
> Root problem: *"The owner has no signal from their dog while they are away, so every decision about the dog's care — crate or no crate, dog walker or not, which routine actually helps — is made in the dark."*

---

## Step 2 — Generate Ideas Across Different Cuts

Apply three different cuts to the root problem.

> **Example — pet owner, separation anxiety:**
>
> **Cut 1 — AI & Software: Real-time emotional check-in app**
> The collar wearable sends continuous emotional state data. The app surfaces a simple read — calm or distressed — updated throughout the day, with a timeline showing when and how long distress episodes lasted. The owner can see at a glance whether their dog has been calm all morning or whether distress spiked for 40 minutes around noon. This directly closes the feedback loop: owners can now make decisions based on real data rather than guesswork.
>
> **Cut 2 — Behavioral pattern layer: Departure pattern detection**
> The app learns the emotional signature around the owner's departure. Does the dog settle quickly, or stay distressed for hours? Over time, it surfaces whether specific departure behaviors (crate, background music, a treat before leaving) are actually helping the dog settle. This moves from *"here is the signal"* to *"here is what the signal means for your specific dog."* Raw data becomes actionable care guidance.
>
> **Cut 3 — Moonshot: A conditioned return signal**
> The dog learns to associate a specific pattern or sound with the owner's return. When the owner is on their way home, they trigger the signal from the app. Not language — a learned cue paired with a positive emotional state. Reduces the dog's uncertainty on the other side. The technology doesn't need to translate precise intent to make this work; it just needs reliable positive/negative emotional tagging.

**Why these are genuinely distinct:**
- Cut 1 creates a new information channel
- Cut 2 adds interpretation to that channel
- Cut 3 uses the channel to communicate *back* to the dog

> **The fastest test for genuine distinctiveness:** Can each idea fail independently? If idea B would only fail if idea A also fails, they are not different ideas. Real distinct options have different risk profiles and different success conditions.

---

## Step 3 — Prioritize Against the Metric and Mission

Pick one and make the rationale work on three levels:

1. **Root problem alignment** — which idea most directly addresses the root problem you named?
2. **Feasibility** — which is buildable given your assumptions about team and timeline?
3. **Metric connection** — which best maps to the north star metric you set in Step 2?

> **Example:**
>
> The **real-time check-in** is the right starting point:
> - Most directly addresses the root problem (no feedback loop)
> - Fully buildable with vague emotional signals — you don't need precise thought translation, just reliable calm vs. distressed
> - Creates a daily habit that generates the data needed to build everything else
> - Maps directly to the north star metric (accuracy of emotional state interpretation validated against observed behavior)
>
> **Departure detection** is compelling but requires enough longitudinal data to establish a per-dog baseline — a second-order product.
>
> **The moonshot** is the longer arc. Naming both as *"later"* and explaining why specifically are part of the signal — they show you're thinking in product stages, not just listing.

> **Tip — always include a moonshot:** Interviewers are not expecting you to build it. They're checking whether you have a longer product arc in mind. *"Here is what this becomes in three years if the signal gets more precise"* signals product vision. Stopping at the *"safe first"* idea signals an execution orientation.

> **Pressure test:** Ask yourself — *"Does solving this completely move my north star metric?"* If yes, you're on track. If *"maybe, eventually,"* you may be solving a supporting problem instead of the core one.

## Leveling Tips

| Level | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| **Solid** | Generates three ideas, picks the most feasible one, explains why it addresses the problem directly. The ideas are reasonable and the recommendation is defensible. |
| **Senior+** | Uses different product cuts to generate ideas that are genuinely distinct in approach — three different *theories* of how to solve the problem. Explains the conceptual difference between each direction before comparing them, so the eventual choice feels argued rather than assumed. The winning idea is connected back to the north star metric and mission from Step 2. Deprioritized ideas are treated as a conditional roadmap, with a clear articulation of what would need to be true for each one to become the right call. |

---

## Common Pitfalls

**Three variations of the same idea.**
*"An app, and also a widget, and also a push notification"* are not three different solutions. They are three delivery formats for the same idea. Switch product cuts deliberately to force genuine distinction.

**Ideas that don't connect to the root problem.**
It's easy to drift toward exciting ideas that solve a related but different problem. Before proposing each idea, check: *"Does this attack the root problem I named at the start of this step?"* If the answer is *"sort of,"* sharpen or replace it.

**Prioritization that ignores the metric.**
Picking the *"most impactful"* idea without connecting it to the north star from Step 2 makes the prioritization feel arbitrary. Explicitly name the metric and show how the winning idea moves it. This is the connective tissue that makes the whole answer feel coherent rather than modular.
