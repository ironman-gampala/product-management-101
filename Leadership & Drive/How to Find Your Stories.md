# How to Find Your Stories

> You do not need a unique answer for every possible behavioral question. That approach is inefficient, it does not scale, and it produces shallow, forgettable responses. Ten well-chosen stories can cover the vast majority of questions you will face.

The key is choosing the right ten and tagging them so you can flex each story into three to five different question types. That's how you'll get the 20% of stories that cover 80% of the questions.

---

## Step 1 — Choose Your 5 Flagship Projects

These are easy to choose because they are the most objective. Ask yourself: *"What are my 5 most impressive projects?"*

- The work you are proudest of — widest appeal, clearest impact
- Projects where you made real decisions, drove real outcomes, and can speak in detail about the tradeoffs you navigated
- Pick projects that span different types of challenges for range

| Story Type | What to Look For |
|---|---|
| **Zero-to-one launch** | A product or capability that didn't exist before you |
| **Complex optimization** | A meaningful improvement to something already in production |
| **Product turnaround** | A struggling product you diagnosed and steered toward success |
| **Data-driven pivot** | A moment where the data changed your direction and you acted on it |
| **Cross-functional initiative** | A project that required you to coordinate across multiple teams or orgs |

---

## Step 2 — Choose Your 5 People / Leadership Stories

These cover the human side of the job. Interviewers are looking for specific signals, so your five people/leadership stories should come from specific categories:

| Category | What Interviewers Are Looking For |
|---|---|
| **Conflict with a stakeholder above you** | Can you navigate upward disagreement without folding or going around someone? |
| **Conflict with a peer or XFN partner** | Can you resolve lateral tension without escalating unnecessarily? |
| **A failure or mistake** | For below Senior: a learning moment. For Senior+: a high-stakes miss with real consequences. The bigger the failure, the more credible you are. |
| **Mentorship or developing others** | Especially important for Senior+ — your ability to grow the people around you is a core evaluation criterion. |
| **Receiving negative feedback and changing because of it** | Tests self-awareness. Best answers: specific feedback, honest reaction, concrete behavior change afterward. |

---

## Step 3 — Use Your Archive as a Memory Jogger

Your first instinct will be to brainstorm stories from memory. Do that — but don't stop there.

Your current resume has been edited ruthlessly. Every update trimmed experiences, compressed bullets, and cut whole roles to fit the page. **The stories you dropped are not gone. They are buried in older versions.** Resumes from three or four jobs ago often contain strong material because the projects were bigger or harder.

**Create a Google Drive folder and add everything:**
- Every version of your resume you have ever had
- LinkedIn profile (export as PDF from LinkedIn settings)
- Promotion packets or promo writeups
- Performance review self-assessments
- Brag docs, impact summaries, or 360 feedback you've received
- Strategy docs, product specs, or launch writeups that capture a decision you owned

> **Tip:** Do not edit before you upload. A rough, messy performance review self-assessment with imprecise language is more useful than a polished one-pager. The raw material is where the real stories live.

Go back through these documents with your ten-story framework in mind. Look for projects you forgot about, conflicts you glossed over, failures you buried.

---

## Step 4 — Check Your Level

A story might be well-told, specific, and genuine — and still signal the wrong level.

Every level up in seniority corresponds to a wider circle of impact:

```
L3: Feature-level impact
L4: Problem-level impact (own the full initiative)
L5: Team-level impact (own the roadmap and direction)
L6: Org-level impact (build new capabilities, cross-org influence)
L7: Cross-org / company-level impact (identify the problems no one assigned you)
```

After picking your initial ten stories, audit each one:

> *"Does this story land at the right altitude for the role I'm targeting?"*

If any stories feel thin or underscoped, they either need excavation or they need a replacement.

---

## Step 5 — Excavate the Buried Details

This is the step that separates a good story bank from a great one. Some of your strongest stories are not fully formed in your head yet. You lived through them, but you have never had to articulate what actually happened at the level of detail an interviewer needs.

Open Claude, ChatGPT, or sit down with a friend. Give them this prompt:

```
I want to dig deeper into this specific project. Here are my raw notes and memories:

[Paste everything you remember — messy is fine]

Ask me questions to help flesh out the full story. I am specifically looking for:

- What was the impact?
- What was the key decision on this project?
- What approach did I choose and why?
- What alternative did I end up not choosing?
- What tradeoffs did I consider?
- Who were my stakeholders, and who did I influence?
- What were the moments of tension in that collaboration?
- How did I drive alignment when there was genuine disagreement?
- Did any process, roadmap, or strategic changes come from this?
- What would I do differently now?
```

Let the AI or your partner interview you. Answer the questions in whatever order they come. The questions force you to articulate things you have internalized but never had to explain out loud.

This is especially helpful for:
- Stories where you suspect there is higher-level impact than how you've described them
- Stories where you can't remember many details

## Step 6 — Tag Your Stories for Modularity

This is where your ten stories become fifty answers. The tagging system turns a small story bank into comprehensive coverage.

Each story gets tagged with attributes that describe its underlying themes. When you hear a question in an interview, you're not searching for "the right story" — you're matching tags.

**The 15 tags that come up most often in PM interviews at top companies:**

| Tag | When It's Needed |
|---|---|
| Conflict or stakeholder tension | Disagreement questions |
| Data-driven decision | Analytical judgment questions |
| Heavy cross-functional collaboration | Teamwork / influence questions |
| High complexity / technical depth | Complex project questions |
| Trade-offs under constraint | Prioritization and decision questions |
| Ambiguity / incomplete information | Working with uncertainty questions |
| Influence without authority | Lateral leadership questions |
| 0-to-1 / greenfield | New product / launch questions |
| Took ownership nobody assigned | Drive and initiative questions |
| Realizing you were wrong / failure | Growth and self-awareness questions |
| Customer obsession / user empathy | Product sense crossover questions |
| Multiple teams or orgs involved | Scale and org complexity questions |
| Receiving or giving difficult feedback | Culture fit and candor questions |
| Shipped under pressure / cut scope | Execution under constraint questions |
| Mentorship / people development | Leadership depth questions |

**Each story should carry 3–5 tags.** Ten stories × 3–5 tags each = 30–50 possible story-question matches. That is coverage.

> **The modularity advantage:** The best candidates do not sound rehearsed because they are not reciting a scripted answer — they are pulling from a story they know cold and shaping the telling to match the specific question. The same project story might emphasize collaboration when asked about teamwork, emphasize data when asked about decision-making, and emphasize conflict when asked about disagreements. One story, three different framings.

## Step 7 — Validate with AI

Once you have your ten stories tagged, use AI to check for gaps:

```
Here are my 10 behavioral stories for PM interviews, with their tags:

[Paste your story list with tags]

I am targeting [level] at [type of company].

Do three things:
1. Flag any common behavioral question themes that my current bank does not cover well.
2. For each story, tell me whether the impact level matches my target or reads lower than it should.
3. Suggest any experiences from my archive that I might be overlooking.
```

This is AI as a second pair of eyes — not as the architect. You picked the stories. AI validates them.

---

## Step 8 — Adapt for Each Company

Your core story bank is company-agnostic. When targeting a specific role, create a separate Claude Project or conversation and run this prompt:

```
I am interviewing for [role title] at [company]. Here is the job description:

[Paste full JD]

Do two things:

1. Research [company] deeply. I want to understand:
   - Their current strategy and priorities
   - Recent product bets or organizational shifts
   - What this specific role signals about what they are optimizing for
   - Culture signals (values, leadership principles, known interview patterns)

2. Based on your research and the JD, write an "ideal candidate profile":
   A concrete description of the person they are trying to hire.
   What experiences would this person have?
   What behaviors would stand out?
   What impact level and scope would their stories demonstrate?
   Be specific enough that I can use this as a filter.
```

Take that profile and hold it up against your ten stories. Which 5–6 best match this company? Which tags should you emphasize? Are there stories that need to shift in emphasis to land differently for this audience?

> **Name your company-specific notes clearly:** *"Story Bank Notes — [Company] — [Month Year]"* so you can track how your stories evolved across different searches. Save them back to your archive folder. Over time the archive builds, new roles get added, stories get stronger. The prep compounds.

## Your Assignment

Complete these steps to build the story bank that covers 80% of questions with 20% of the prep:

- [ ] Brainstorm your ten stories from memory: five flagship projects, five people/leadership stories
- [ ] Go through your resume archive and look for stories you forgot or undervalued
- [ ] Audit each story against the level scale — does it match your target?
- [ ] Excavate any story that feels thin or underscoped — let AI or a friend interview you about it
- [ ] Tag each story with 3–5 of the 15 tags
- [ ] Run the validation prompt to check for gaps
- [ ] When you have a specific company target, run the company research prompt and filter your bank

By the end, you should have ten stories you know deeply, tagged for flexibility, and ready to be practiced.
