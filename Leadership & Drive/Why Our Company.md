# "Why Our Company?"

> Every interview will almost surely include a variation of this question — *"Why do you want to work here?"*, *"Why are you a good fit?"*, *"Why Netflix?"* A question which seems simple on its surface can, if approached naively, expose you as unprepared, too junior, or both.

---

## The Question Beneath the Question

A literal interpretation of *"Why Netflix?"* is *"Why do you want to work at Netflix?"* It is harmful to approach it this way.

| Literal Interpretation | The Real Question |
|---|---|
| *"Here is how Netflix can help me"* | *"What do you know about us, and how can you help?"* |

When you answer literally, you signal that the company is a vehicle for your ambitions. When you answer the real question, you show that you have done your homework and can connect your specific skills to their specific needs.

**The formula:** Tell a brief, compelling story that connects what you know about them to what you can do for them.

---

## Ugly, Bad, and Good — Annotated Examples

*(All examples assume a senior machine learning engineering interview at Netflix.)*

---

### Ugly — All About Me

> *"That's a great question. I want to work at Netflix because I have always been passionate about accessible products that have as big of an impact as possible, just like how Netflix's mission is to entertain the world."*

**Why it's ugly:**
- Answers the question literally — it's about what Netflix can do for the candidate
- No demonstration of research done
- No connection between the candidate's skills and what Netflix actually needs
- Generic enough to be said to any company in any industry

---

### Bad — Telling Without Showing

> *"I believe that Netflix's cutting-edge technology, driven culture, and unique structure make it an ideal place to grow professionally and make an impact. I am also passionate about being part of a team that is constantly pushing boundaries and creating compelling content for viewers around the world. There is so much expertise in machine learning at Netflix, and the team members are very impressive. My background in causal inference fits quite well, and I'm confident I can make an impact quickly."*

**Why it's bad:**
- Attempts to answer the question beneath the question — which is better — but fails to execute
- Tells rather than shows: claims to have done research without demonstrating it
- No metrics, no scope, no impact statements
- Mentions the candidate's background without connecting it to a specific problem Netflix needs solved
- Could still be said at almost any ML-forward company

---

### Good — Connects Knowledge to Value

> *"I recently read a research article Netflix put out called 'Inferring the Long-Term Causal Effects of Long-Term Treatments from Short-Term Experiments' and was energized by the research and findings — specifically the inclusion of offline reinforcement learning methods.*
>
> *You and I both know it won't be A/B tests that get Netflix's algorithms to their full potential — it's going to be causal inference that does that. Netflix describes itself on its website as a 'pioneer of causal inference.' So Netflix needs sharp, independent ML engineers skilled in causal inference to continue leading the entertainment industry.*
>
> *My expertise within machine learning is in causal inference. In my current role, where I lead a team of 4–5 ML engineers, I run causal inference tests and present findings to CMOs, VPs, and technical audiences. These tests have generated over $10 million in annual revenue, primarily in targeted advertising.*
>
> *Netflix would be a solid match for my skillset, and I'd appreciate the opportunity to further my expertise in causal inference."*

**Why it works:**
- Opens with a specific artifact of research (an actual Netflix paper) — demonstrates homework done, not just claimed
- Frames the company's need clearly before connecting to the candidate's skills
- Includes metrics ($10M revenue) and leadership scope (team of 4–5)
- The answer could not be given at any other company — it's specific to Netflix's actual research output and stated positioning
- Closes by pointing the value toward them, not toward personal gain

## The "Why Our Company" Formula for PMs

```
1. ONE SPECIFIC THING YOU FOUND IN YOUR RESEARCH
   → A product decision, a strategic move, a published paper, an earnings call comment,
     a feature you noticed, a recent hire, a leadership principle in action
   → Something specific enough that it couldn't be said at a competitor

2. CONNECT THEIR NEED TO YOUR SKILL
   → What problem does that signal they're trying to solve?
   → What specifically in your background maps to that problem?

3. ANCHOR WITH IMPACT AND SCOPE
   → Drop one metric or scope signal (team size, revenue, users, etc.)
   → This is where you score senior-level points

4. CLOSE POINTING VALUE TOWARD THEM
   → Not "I would grow" but "I can help you do X"
```

**Example PM version:**

> *"I've been following how [Company] is approaching [specific product area] — particularly [specific decision, launch, or strategy signal]. What stood out to me is that you're betting on [insight about their direction], which most competitors haven't done yet.*
>
> *That's exactly the problem I've spent the last two years working on. At [Current Company], I led [initiative] for a product with [scale or user context]. We [specific outcome with a metric]. The hardest part was [challenge that connects to their problem] — which taught me [transferable insight].*
>
> *That's why this role specifically makes sense — not just [Company] in general, but this problem at this moment."*

## Quick Reference

```
HEAR THIS:    "Why our company?"
ANSWER THIS:  "What do you know about us, and how can you help?"

STRUCTURE
→ One specific thing from your research (article, product decision, earnings call, strategy signal)
→ Frame their need — what problem does that signal they're solving?
→ Connect your specific skill or experience to that specific need
→ Anchor with one metric or scope signal
→ Close toward their value, not your ambition

SIGNALS THAT GET YOU DOWNLEVELED
→ Generic passion statements ("I've always loved your mission")
→ Culture praise that applies to every top company ("brilliant people, growth mindset...")
→ No research demonstrated — just claims of research
→ No metrics or scope — could be a junior candidate's answer
→ Closing on what you'll gain, not what you'll deliver

SIGNALS THAT SCORE SENIOR POINTS
→ Reference to a specific artifact: paper, product decision, strategy shift, or exec quote
→ Named the problem they need solved before naming yourself as the solution
→ Impact metric from your own work that maps to their challenge
→ The answer could not be said at any other company
```
