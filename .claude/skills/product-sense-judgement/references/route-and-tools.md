# Route and tools

The tools behind each step of the 30-minute route. Each one exists to stop a specific freeze or a
specific point loss. Teach the tool, not just the answer, so the learner can reuse it on any question.

## 1. Clarify (0–3 min)

Ask 3–4 questions **in one batch**, then state assumptions. Asking one question at a time can
eat six minutes. Only ask questions whose answer would change the approach.

Good clarifiers:
- Why is the company looking at this now?
- Which market / geography?
- Which part of the product (for broad prompts like "improve Spotify")?
- Timeline and team size?
- Any constraint (platform, regulation, budget)?

Opening line: *"Before I dive in, I'd like to ask a few quick questions so I solve the right problem."*
Closing line: *"Great. I'll assume ___, ___ and ___. Let me move on to why this matters for the company."*

If the interviewer answers a question, play it back in one line rather than asking it again.

## 2. Strategy and north star (3–6 min)

Three parts, in order, about 30 seconds each:

```
  MISSION        what the company exists to do (in plain words)
  WHY THIS CO    what they gain from solving this (trust, usage, data, revenue, moat)
  WHY NOW        what changed: competitor, cost, regulation, behaviour, technology
  NORTH STAR     ONE number, said in one sentence, written on the board
```

North star tests:
- **One number**, not a count and a rate mixed together.
- **Measurable from a real signal** (what does the phone / server actually see?).
- **Tied to the pain** (if the pain is time lost, time belongs in the metric).
- **Measures the outcome, not the method**, so it survives when the solution changes.
- **Scoped honestly** (e.g. "trips to hard-to-park areas", not all trips).

Example: *"The share of trips to hard-to-park areas where the user parks within 3 minutes of
arriving, without circling."*

Defending it out loud is a strength: *"Does this still work if we later add booking? Yes — it
measures time to park, not how they found the spot."*

## 3. Users (6–11 min)

Don't search memory for users. Run two lenses and the users fall out:

```
  WHY are they using it?     (purpose: work, leisure, errands, events, learning…)
  HOW OFTEN?                 (daily vs once in a while)
  WHAT / WHERE?              (device or vehicle; place or context)
```

Say: *"Let me split users by why they use it and how often."*

Format: 3–4 users, **one line each** (who + why). Then pick one with two reasons, and one line on
why not the obvious runner-up. Set aside extras in one sentence. Save the stories for pain points.

Good pick reasons: size of group, severity of pain, frequency (habit), fit with the company's edge.
Good reject reasons: "their problem solves itself after a week", "rare", "a different problem".

For B2B, see `b2b.md` first.

## 4. Pain points (11–16 min)

```
  a. GOAL               what is this person really trying to get done?
  b. JOURNEY            walk their experience stage by stage
  c. PAINS              3+ problems found along the journey, one line each
  d. PICK ONE           how often × how bad (+ "fixing it helps the others")
  e. PROBLEM STATEMENT  one sentence with a "because"
```

Journey walk (adapt the stages to the product):
```
  BEFORE → APPROACH / START → CORE MOMENT → FINISH → AFTER / RETURN
```
Asking "what goes wrong here?" at each stage produces pains without needing inspiration.

Give the user a name and a concrete scene ("Priya, Saturday night, a new restaurant in
Indiranagar"). It makes pains vivid and easy to follow.

Problem statement template:
> *"[User] who [situation] struggles to [pain] because [root cause], which leads to [cost]."*

The **because** clause is what the solutions will attack — that's what stops the solution freeze.

## 5. Solutions (16–24 min)

### Go wide first
Say: *"Let me first name three different approaches, then pick one and go deep."*

Three lenses, one idea each:
```
  1. INFORMATION   show them something they can't see today
  2. AUTOMATION    do the work for them
  3. INCENTIVE / SOCIAL / PARTNER   change what other people or businesses do
```
Bonus test: is there an **"only we can do this"** idea that uses the company's unique data or scale?

Number them out loud: "My first idea… my second… my third…". When the learner hears themselves say
"and there's another option where…", that is a new idea — number it.

### Pick
One pick with reasons tied to the problem statement. Strong reason patterns:
- "It goes straight at the root cause in my problem statement."
- "It's the foundation the other ideas depend on."
- "It moves the north star most for the least effort."
Then reject the others in one line each.

### Walk the six-step flow
```
  ENTRY              where does the user first meet it?
  SCREEN             what do they see?
  ACTION             what do they tap / do, and what happens next?
  BEHIND THE SCENES  where does the data / logic come from?
  RESULT             what is different for the user, with a number?
  EDGE CASE          what if it goes wrong, and how do we stay honest?
```
One or two sentences each. End with a closing line about the user's changed experience — never
"so yeah".

## 6. MVP and metrics (24–28 min)

```
  PREREQUISITE   what must be TRUE before launch (data accuracy, permission, partner, policy)
  v1             smallest version that still delivers the value; scope (where, who, which cases)
  WHAT WAITS     each cut idea + why it waits (dependency, cost, risk, needs data)
  METRICS        north star (read it off the board) + supporting + 2 guardrails
```

Guardrail = something that could get worse while the north star improves. Ask: *"If my north star
goes up, what could I be breaking without noticing?"* Examples: accuracy/trust, door-to-door time,
opt-outs, cost per user, fairness. The north star flipped around is not a guardrail.

Name one second-order effect, and one owner + first 72-hour action + reversal line when there's time.

## 7. Summary (28–30 min)

*"To summarise: for [user], the real problem is [problem]. I'd build [solution] first because
[reason], measure it with [north star], and [next step] comes after."* Thirty seconds, no new ideas.

## 8. Transition lines

Saying the next step's name out loud buys thinking time and sounds structured:
- "Before I dive in, I'd like to ask a few quick questions."
- "Let me start with why this matters for [company]."
- "Let me split users by why they use it and how often."
- "Let me walk through their journey and see where it goes wrong."
- "Let me first name three different approaches, then pick one."
- "Let me walk through how it works, from entry to result."
- "Before launch, the one thing that has to be true is…"
- "To summarise…"

## 9. Freeze recovery

| Freeze | What to say | What to do in your head |
|---|---|---|
| Can't find more users | "Let me think about this by purpose and frequency." | Run the user lenses |
| Can't find pains | "Let me walk through their day." | Journey stages, ask "what goes wrong here?" |
| Stuck on one solution | "That's one approach. Let me look at it from a different angle." | Run the other two lenses |
| Lost the thread | "Let me quickly recap where we are." | Read the board top to bottom |
| Filler creeping in | — | Pause one silent second, think the first word of the step |

## 10. Common point losses

- Listing options without picking (no judgement shown).
- Skipping "why this company" and jumping to the metric.
- Mixing pain stories into the users step.
- Detailing idea 1 before naming ideas 2 and 3.
- Stopping the flow at the screen.
- Putting into v1 something you argued must come later.
- Using the north star flipped as a guardrail.
- Ending a turn with "so yeah" or "that's it".
- Asking the interviewer for help ("please help me with others") instead of using a lens out loud.
