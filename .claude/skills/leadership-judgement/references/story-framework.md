# Story framework

The tools behind a strong behavioural answer. Each one exists to stop a specific point loss. Teach
the tool, not just the answer, so the learner can reuse it on any question and any story.

## 1. STAR+ beat by beat

```
  OPEN    headline first        "I [did X] and [Y changed by Z]."
  S       situation + stakes    only the context needed to follow; why it mattered; what was at risk
  T       task                  my job — and whether I was handed it or found it
  A       action                what I did, built around ONE key decision
  R       result                numbers + what changed beyond the metric
  +       reflection            what I learned / would do differently, as a transferable insight
```

**Situation.** Two or three sentences. Cut the company history, the org chart and the product tour.
Keep one line of stakes: a deadline, a number going the wrong way, an exec escalation, a customer
about to leave.

**Task.** Say what was yours. "My job was to decide whether we launch in March" is clear. "We needed
to improve retention" hides you. At L5+, say if you spotted the problem yourself.

**Action.** This is 50–60% of the time. Structure it as:
- **The key decision:** what I chose and why.
- **The alternative:** what I didn't do, and why not.
- **The trade-off:** what I gave up (speed, scope, a relationship, a metric).
- **The hardest moment:** where the plan was tested — resistance, a surprise, a near-miss — and what I did.

Say "I" for your own calls. Use "we" only for things the team truly did together, and then say your
part in it.

**Result.** Baseline → change → time frame ("from 31% to 22% in six weeks"). Add scale (users,
revenue, teams) and a second-order change: a process adopted, funding unlocked, a team re-planned.
If the result was bad, say so plainly — that is a failure story, and it can still score well.

**+ (reflection).** One or two sentences. Not "communication is important". A transferable insight
that says how you work now: "I now agree the success metric with the sceptic before the build, not
after the launch."

## 2. From STAR+ to a story: anchors, stakes and tension

Scripts sound rehearsed and break under follow-ups. Memorise **three anchors** per story and tell
the middle naturally:

```
  ANCHOR 1  OPENING   1–2 sentences, bottom line up front (project, result, impact)
  ANCHOR 2  CLOSING   1–2 sentences on what CHANGED — not just the metric
  ANCHOR 3  THREE CAN'T-MISS SCENES
            1. The stakes        why it mattered, how hard it was
            2. The key decision  what I chose, what I rejected, the trade-off
            3. The challenge     the moment the plan was tested, and what I did
```

How the anchors map to STAR+: the opening is the headline; scene 1 is S + T; scenes 2 and 3 are A;
the closing is R + the "+".

**Stakes and tension** are what make a story memorable. A story with no tension is a status update.
Test: if nobody disagreed, nothing was at risk and there was only one sensible option, pick a
different story.

**Change over time.** Something must be different because of you — the product, the team, the org,
or how you think. The closing says what.

**Flex the same story.** Keep the opening and closing; shift which scene gets the time:

| Question | Emphasise |
|---|---|
| Influence without authority | Scenes 2 + 3 — who resisted, what they protected, how you changed their mind |
| Tough trade-off / prioritisation | Scene 2 — what you chose not to build and why |
| Delivered under pressure | Scenes 1 + 3 — the deadline, what went sideways, what you cut |
| Data-driven decision | Scene 2 — what the data said, what it couldn't tell you, how you decided anyway |
| Failure | Scene 3 + the "+" — what you got wrong, what you changed afterwards |

**Five-point quality check** before a story is interview-ready: Does the opening hook? Does
something change? Is it about you (80% of the words)? Would it hold attention at a dinner table? Is
the scope right for the target level?

**The 10-minute test:** if they can't answer follow-ups for 10 minutes without hesitating, the story
needs excavating, not better wording.

## 3. Amazon vs everyone else

- **Amazon:** clean STAR, very specific, data-heavy, one leadership principle per story. Expect "what
  did *you* do?" and "what was the data?" repeatedly. Use "I". Say the result with numbers.
- **Meta, Google, Netflix, frontier AI labs:** narrative arc with stakes and tension, a real decision
  at the centre, honest reflection. Follow-ups go 3–4 levels deep. Netflix probes candour and
  feedback; AI labs like constraint-heavy stories (incomplete information, pressure to ship).

The beats are identical. For Amazon, signpost them more; elsewhere, let them flow as a story.

## 4. Level signals

The scope of the story signals the level before anything else does.

| Level | Scope of the story | Ownership | Strategy : execution | Closing should show |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L3 (APM) | A feature or bounded task | Given a clear task, did it well | ~20 : 80 | What you learned |
| L4 (PM / PM2) | A problem end to end, cross-functional | Owned the initiative | ~30 : 70 | How it changed your approach |
| L5 (Senior) | The team's roadmap or direction | Shaped the direction, pushed back on asks | ~40 : 60 | Team-level change + transferable insight |
| L6 (Staff) | An org-level capability, several teams | Built something new, cross-org influence | ~60 : 40 | Org-level change, a principle |
| L7 (Principal) | A company problem nobody owned | Found it, got the mandate, drove it | ~80 : 20 | How the company operates differently |

**Five level signals to audit in every story:**
1. **Scope:** feature → problem → team → org → company.
2. **Ownership:** assigned to you → identified by you.
3. **Stakeholders:** named role + what they resisted + how you changed their mind.
4. **Delivery:** calm, specific, conversational — a colleague, not a performer.
5. **Strategy ratio:** how much of the story is *why* and *what*, vs *how*.

**Senior tells:** you name the decision and the rejected alternative unprompted; you talk about the
people and what they were optimising for; you mention go-to-market or org effects, not just the
build; you close with a principle.

**Lead with your biggest story.** First impressions anchor the interviewer's calibration. A
feature-level first answer makes them grade the rest of the round lower.

## 5. Question families and which stories answer them

A bank of 6–8 stories should cover every family at least once, and most families twice.

| Family | Typical wording | Best story type | What they listen for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conflict with eng / a peer | "Tell me about a conflict with a stakeholder" | Cross-functional initiative; conflict with peer | Named person, what they protected, how you resolved it without escalating |
| Conflict with leadership | "A time you disagreed with your manager / leadership" | Conflict above you | Backbone with respect; data, not volume; the outcome either way |
| Disagree and commit | "You disagreed but went ahead anyway" | Conflict above you, lost the argument | Argued clearly, then committed fully; what happened next |
| Failure / mistake | "Biggest failure", "confident and later wrong" | A real miss with consequences | Plain ownership, no blame, what you changed afterwards |
| Influence without authority | "Convinced a team that didn't report to you" | Cross-functional or multi-org | How you changed minds: data, their incentives, a small proof |
| Ambiguous 0→1 | "Built something from scratch", "incomplete information" | Zero-to-one launch | How you framed the problem, what you bet on first, how you de-risked |
| Hard prioritisation / saying no | "A time you said no", "tough trade-off" | Turnaround or roadmap reset | What you cut, who was upset, why it was right |
| Data-driven decision | "Data changed your mind", "worked with incomplete data" | Data-driven pivot | What the data said, what it couldn't say, the decision you made |
| Delivering under pressure | "Tight deadline", "delay a launch under exec pressure" | Shipped under pressure / cut scope | What you cut and why, the risk you accepted, the line you wouldn't cross |
| Ownership / drive | "Took on something not your job", "unblocked a stalled project" | Took ownership nobody assigned | The risk you took on, staying in it when it got hard |
| Most impactful / complex project | "Project you're proudest of" | Your biggest flagship | Scope, decisions across the lifecycle — this becomes a deep dive |
| Feedback received | "Negative feedback you got" | Feedback story | Specific feedback, honest reaction, concrete behaviour change |
| Mentoring / developing others | "Grew someone on your team" | Mentorship story | What you saw, what you did, how they changed (key at L5+) |

## 6. Length guide

| Answer | Target | Too long when… |
|---|---|---|
| First telling of a story | 2 minutes (1.5–2.5) | Situation passes 30 seconds |
| Follow-up answer | 20–45 seconds | You re-tell the story instead of answering |
| Experience deep-dive walk-through | 3–4 minutes | You list every feature |
| "Tell me about yourself" | 60–90 seconds, never over 2 minutes | You reach your first job before 30 seconds |
| "Why this company" | 45–60 seconds | It's mostly about what you'll gain |

Spoken English runs about 140–160 words a minute, so a 2-minute story is about 300 words.

## 7. Transition lines

Saying the beat out loud keeps you on track and buys thinking time:
- "The short version: I [headline]. Let me give you the context."
- "What made this hard was…" (stakes)
- "My job was to…" / "Nobody owned this, so I…" (task)
- "The real decision was between A and B." (key decision)
- "I chose A because… The cost was…" (trade-off)
- "The moment it almost fell apart was when…" (challenge)
- "The result was… and what changed beyond that was…" (result)
- "What I'd do differently is…" / "What I took from it is…" (+)

## 8. Freeze recovery

| Freeze | What to say | What to do in your head |
|---|---|---|
| Can't think of a story | "Let me pick the best example — give me a few seconds." | Match the question's family to your bank's tag list |
| Story doesn't quite fit | "The closest example is… and here's how it relates." | Flex the scene emphasis (§2) |
| Lost in the situation | "The short version is…" | Jump to the key decision |
| Can't remember a number | "I don't remember the exact figure; it was roughly X, and the direction was clear." | Never invent precision |
| Hard follow-up ("why not B?") | "Fair question. I considered B, and here's why I didn't pick it." | State the trade-off you made |
| Rambling | — | Stop, say the result, then the one thing you learned |

## 9. Common point losses

- "We" all the way through — the interviewer can't tell what you did.
- No number in the result, or a number with no baseline.
- No tension: everyone agreed, nothing was at risk.
- Activities instead of a decision ("I ran workshops, did research, presented").
- Situation runs past 30 seconds; the headline never comes.
- Story scope below the target level, or leading with the smallest story.
- "I aligned stakeholders" with no names, roles or resistance.
- Defensive or disguised failure ("my weakness is I work too hard"); blaming another team.
- Reflection is a platitude ("communication is key").
- Trailing off ("so yeah, that's it") instead of landing the closing line.
- Telling a good story to a different question.
- Sounding scripted: no spontaneous detail, no honest uncertainty.
